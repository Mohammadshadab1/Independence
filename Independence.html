Why we celebrate

A midnight built on a century of struggle

Independence Day marks the end of nearly 200 years of British colonial rule in India and the birth of a sovereign nation. It commemorates not one event but generations of resistance — mass movements, quiet defiance, and the work of framing a republic that would govern itself.

Each year on 15 August, the Prime Minister hoists the national flag at the Red Fort in Delhi and addresses the nation, a tradition that has continued since 1947.

The road to freedom

Ninety years, in six turns

1857

The first major uprising

Widespread revolt against East India Company rule spreads across northern and central India, marking the first large-scale challenge to colonial authority. Begum Hazrat Mahal led the revolt in Awadh.

1885

Indian National Congress founded

The Congress forms as a political platform for Indians to voice demands for greater self-governance, later becoming the vehicle of the independence movement.

1919

Jallianwala Bagh

Troops open fire on an unarmed gathering in Amritsar, killing hundreds. The massacre hardens public opinion and fuels the non-cooperation movement that follows.

1930

The Salt March

Mahatma Gandhi leads a 24-day march to the coast at Dandi to defy the salt tax, turning civil disobedience into a mass movement.

1942

Quit India

The Congress launches a call for an immediate end to British rule, sparking nationwide protests and mass arrests of its leadership. Maulana Abul Kalam Azad was a key voice in this movement.

1947

Midnight, 15 August

India becomes a sovereign nation. Jawaharlal Nehru is sworn in as the first Prime Minister, and the tricolour is hoisted at the Red Fort for the first time.

Meet our heroes

The people who won it

Tap a card to read about each freedom fighter — Hindus, Muslims, Sikhs and people of all faiths who fought together for one India.

Mahatma Gandhi

Non-violent resistance

Led the Salt March and the Quit India Movement, shaping satyagraha — non-violent civil disobedience — as a tool for national resistance. His philosophy of truth and non-violence inspired the world.

Bhagat Singh

Revolutionary

A young revolutionary whose defiance and execution at age 23 turned him into a lasting symbol of fearless sacrifice for the motherland.

Subhas Chandra Bose

Indian National Army

Founded the Azad Hind government-in-exile and led the Indian National Army in the armed struggle for independence. Gave the nation the slogan "Jai Hind."

Sarojini Naidu

Poet & freedom fighter

A poet and orator who became a leading voice of the movement and, in 1947, the first woman governor of an Indian state. Known as the "Nightingale of India."

Jawaharlal Nehru

First Prime Minister

Delivered the iconic "Tryst with Destiny" speech on the midnight of independence. As India's first PM, he laid the foundations of a modern, secular republic.

Rani Lakshmibai

Queen of Jhansi

The Queen of Jhansi who led her army against the British during the 1857 uprising, becoming a timeless symbol of resistance, courage and women's leadership.

Tipu Sultan

Tiger of Mysore

The 18th-century ruler of Mysore who fought four Anglo-Mysore Wars against the British. He pioneered rocket artillery and refused to submit to colonial expansion.

Maulana Abul Kalam Azad

Scholar & Statesman

India's first Education Minister and the youngest Congress president. A scholar who advocated Hindu-Muslim unity, opposed Partition, and shaped India's education policy.

Ashfaqulla Khan

Revolutionary Poet

A revolutionary of the Kakori train conspiracy and a poet who dreamed of a free India. Hanged by the British at age 27, he remains a symbol of Muslim contribution to the freedom struggle.

Begum Hazrat Mahal

Leader of 1857

Led the 1857 revolt in Awadh after her husband was exiled. She rallied forces against the British, refused all compromises, and symbolised women's leadership in the first war of independence.

The flag

What every band of the Tiranga means

Saffron

Represents courage, sacrifice and the spirit of renunciation shown by the country's freedom fighters — from Tipu Sultan's wars to the Quit India Movement.

White & the Ashoka Chakra

White stands for truth and peace. At its centre, a navy-blue wheel of 24 spokes — the Ashoka Chakra — represents the eternal wheel of law, taken from the Lion Capital at Sarnath.

Green

Signifies the land's fertility, growth and prosperity — the promise of a nation that continues to grow 80 years after freedom.

National symbols

Emblems of the republic

Anthem

Jana Gana Mana

Written by Rabindranath Tagore; adopted as the national anthem in 1950.

National song

Vande Mataram

Composed by Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay, a rallying song of the independence movement.

Emblem

Lion Capital of Ashoka

Adapted from the Sarnath pillar; adopted as the state emblem in 1950.

National flower

Lotus

Symbolises purity and resilience, rising unstained from murky water.

National bird

Indian Peacock

Known for its grace and colour, declared the national bird in 1963.

National animal

Royal Bengal Tiger

Chosen for its strength and majesty; also the anchor species of India's tiger reserves.

Flying it right

The Flag Code, in short

Since 2002, every citizen has the right to fly the national flag with dignity, within a few simple rules.

  • 1Hoist it saffron-side up — the flag must never be flown upside down.
  • 2Keep it clean and whole; a torn or soiled flag should not be flown.
  • 3Never let it touch the ground or water beneath it.
  • 4It may be flown day and night if properly illuminated.
  • 5When retired, it should be disposed of privately and with dignity.
Jai Hind

Wherever you are on 15 August, take a moment to raise it — even if it's just a hand over your heart.

Eighty years in, the tricolour still means the same three things it always has: courage, peace, and the promise of growth.
